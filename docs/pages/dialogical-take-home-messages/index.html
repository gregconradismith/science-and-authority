I hope this course has provided you with an opportunity to think about science & society and the meaning of science.

To conclude these blog pages and wrap things up ... recall that we began our exploration of Science & Authority with Max Weber's concern that science does not inform us about the meaning of the world (What should we do? How should we live?). Nevertheless, many of us are committed to scientific values such as honesty, communalism, objectivity, and skepticism.

I know that science does not provide any insight into normative questions such as “How shall I live?".

I will uphold scientific values such as honesty, communalism, objectivity, and skepticism.

Mulling over the conflicting or paradoxical aspects of these thoughts is a helpful way to consolidate what we have learned this semester. This would be an example of what we have called "dialectical reasoning," thinking in a way that highlights contradictions, opposing viewpoints, or tensions to arrive at a deeper understanding or synthesis of ideas. By embracing opposing viewpoints, dialectical reasoning fosters nuanced thinking.

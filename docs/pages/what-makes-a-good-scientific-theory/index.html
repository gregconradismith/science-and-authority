What makes a good scientific theory?

In addition to testability and falsifiability (ala Karl Popper), several other principles pertain to evaluating a scientific theory or hypothesis. These principles would be part of a more comprehensive approach to the "demarcation problem" (what distinguishes science from non-science). These principles help ensure that a theory or hypothesis is scientifically sound and productive for advancing knowledge.

Simplicity (Parsimony): A good theory should not be more complex than necessary. It should explain phenomena in the most straightforward manner possible while accounting for the relevant data. The principle of parsimony is called Occam's Razor after the medieval philosopher and theologian William of Ockham. Although Ockham did not originate this principle, he is known for frequently using it in his writings. The idea is sometimes summarized as "entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity."

Explanatory Power: A strong theory or hypothesis provides a deep explanation of phenomena, linking various observations under a coherent framework. It should make sense of a wide range of facts or observations.

Predictive Power: Beyond explaining current observations, a good theory should predict future outcomes or new phenomena that can be verified empirically.

The big data approach - as is the fashion these days - has the power to predict phenomena but often does not provide insight into mechanisms. Advocates of the approach may not understand why or how it works?

Consistency: The theory should be internally consistent, with no contradictions in its logic or structure. It should also be consistent with well-established scientific knowledge unless it offers a reasoned basis to challenge it.

Are the current theories of quantum mechanics and gravitation internally consistent? Are they consistent with one another?

Scope: A strong theory has broad applicability, explaining a wide range of phenomena rather than being limited to a narrow set of circumstances.

Fruitfulness: A good theory should stimulate further research, generating new questions and experiments. It should open up new avenues of inquiry and lead to novel discoveries.

Coherence with Existing Knowledge: While a theory should challenge and refine existing paradigms if needed, it should also fit within the broader framework of scientific knowledge or convincingly argue why certain aspects of that framework need revision.

Reproducibility: The theory should lead to results that other scientists can independently verify through experiments or observations.

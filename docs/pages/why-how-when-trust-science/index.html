Why/How/When Trust Science?

Naomi Oreskes - coauthor with Erik M. Conway of Merchants of Doubt - a book that documents how a handful of scientists manufactured doubt on issues ranging from tobacco smoke to climate change - wrote a subsequence book titled Why Trust Science? The publisher's note on the back cover says...

[In Why Trust Science? Naomi Oreskes] offers a bold and compelling defense of science, revealing why the social character of scientific knowledge is its greatest strength - and the greatest reason we can trust it. The trustworthiness of scientific claims derives from the social process by which they are rigorously vetted. This process is not perfect - nothing ever is when humans are involved - but we can learn from cases where scientists got it wrong. Oreskes shows how consensus is a crucial indicator of when a scientific matter has been settled, and when the knowledge produced is likely to be trustworthy. 

In this course on Science & Authority, we have camped out on science gone wrong and social critiques of science, perhaps to a fault.  We have emphasized how science has gone wrong (and continues to go wrong).  We have critiqued the epistemic authority of science. 

I would like to conclude with a focus on Trust.  Why is it that science should be trusted or ... if that is too general ...  What are some practical guidelines for why you - as STEM majors - representing the next generation of American elites - ought to trust science or ... if that is too strong ... Can we at least find some way - in the midst of the critique - to give the better angels of science the respect they are due? In practical terms, Why, How and When Ought We Trust Science?

<p>Karl Popper (1902–1994) proposed falsifiability as the criterion of demarcation of science from nonscience. Popper emphasized that no amount of evidence can ever prove a theory, but a single contradictory observation can refute it. A good theory, according to Popper, makes bold, risky predictions that can be refuted. </p>


<p>In addition to Karl Popper (1902–1994), many philosophers of science have contributed to the demarcation problem (the question of distinguishing science from nonscience) and the criteria for a good scientific theory.  Perhaps the most influential is Thomas Kuhn. </p>


<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Thomas Kuhn  and The Structure of Scientific Revolutions</h2>


<p>Thomas Kuhn's book <em>The Structure of Scientific Revolutions</em> (1962) challenges the linear, cumulative view of scientific progress. Kuhn argues that science does not advance via a steady accumulation of knowledge but rather through a series of discontinuous transformations—"<strong>paradigm shifts</strong>." These shifts occur in the following stages:</p>


<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Normal Science</strong>: Scientists work within an accepted framework or "paradigm" that dictates the scientific community's theories, methods, and standards. This period is characterized by puzzle-solving and refining existing knowledge rather than questioning foundational assumptions.</li>


<li><strong>Anomalies</strong>: Over time, observations that do not fit the paradigm accumulate. While minor anomalies are typically resolved within the paradigm, some are too significant to be ignored.</li>


<li><strong>Crisis</strong>: As more anomalies appear, confidence in the existing paradigm weakens, leading to a crisis period. At this point, the field is more open to radical new theories.</li>


<li><strong>Paradigm Shift (Scientific Revolution)</strong>: A new paradigm is proposed, which fundamentally changes how the scientific community understands its subject matter. This new paradigm is not just an extension of the old; it offers an entirely different framework, altering how scientists view and investigate phenomena.</li>


<li><strong>Post-Revolutionary Science</strong>: The new paradigm becomes dominant, and science returns to "normal" within this new framework until the cycle potentially begins again.</li>
</ul>


<p>Kuhn also emphasizes the "incommensurability" of paradigms, meaning that competing paradigms are often so different that they cannot be directly compared. They involve different questions, methods, and standards of evidence, making it difficult to assess one paradigm using the criteria of another.</p>


<p>The historical significance of Kuhn's work includes a break from positivism and a challenge to Popper's falsificationism. Before Kuhn, the dominant view of science was grounded in logical positivism, which held that science progresses through the accumulation of objective, empirical facts. Kuhn's model disrupted this view by arguing that science is, to some extent, a socially constructed process shaped by the prevailing paradigms. Kuhn pointed out that in "normal science," scientists do not abandon a paradigm simply because anomalies arise. Instead, they work to resolve anomalies within the existing framework, often holding onto the paradigm until a new, more compelling one emerges.</p>


<p>Kuhn's work expanded the understanding of scientific change and introduced the idea that social and historical factors are integral to scientific development. It opened the door for a more nuanced view of scientific practice, emphasizing the role of consensus, tradition, and cultural factors in shaping science.</p>


<p>Many other philosophers of science have contributed to the demarcation problem and the criteria for a good scientific theory. Some of the most influential include:</p>


<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Imre Lakatos (1922–1974). The Research Program Approach </strong></h2>


<p>Lakatos sought to reconcile Kuhn's view of paradigm shifts with Popper's emphasis on falsifiability. He argued that science progresses through competing research programs, each with a "hard core" of fundamental assumptions. A theory's auxiliary hypotheses can be modified to account for anomalies, but the core remains protected. Lakatos introduced the notion of progressive versus degenerative research programs, where progressive programs predict novel facts and accommodate anomalies better, while degenerative ones rely on ad hoc modifications. A theory is better if it leads to progressive research programs that make novel predictions rather than degenerative ones that merely adjust to anomalies without predictive success.</p>


<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Paul Feyerabend (1924–1994). Epistemological anarchism</strong></h2>


<p>Feyerabend was a critic of rigid methodologies in science. In his book Against Method (1975), he argued against the existence of a single scientific method, claiming that "anything goes" in the history of science. He pointed out that many scientific breakthroughs occurred by breaking the established methodological rules. Feyerabend believed that the demarcation problem was unsolvable and that trying to separate science from nonscience rigidly was misleading. Feyerabend was skeptical of criteria for evaluating theories, advocating for a more pluralistic and anarchistic view of science. He believed that scientific progress often involves breaking established rules.</p>


<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Larry Laudan (1941–2022). Problem-solving Effectiveness of Theories</strong></h2>


<p>Laudan rejected the notion of a definitive demarcation criterion between science and nonscience. He argued instead that theories should be evaluated based on their ability to solve "empirical and conceptual problems." For Laudan, a theory is valuable if it helps resolve both practical issues and theoretical inconsistencies. A good theory, according to Laudan, is fruitful in solving a wide range of problems. Rather than focusing on rigid criteria like falsifiability or verification, theories should be judged on their problem-solving success.</p>


<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>W.V.O. Quine (1908–2000). Holism and Underdetermination</strong></h2>


<p>Quine challenged the sharp distinction between analytic (logical) and synthetic (empirical) truths, proposing instead that scientific theories form a "web of beliefs" where no statement can be conclusively falsified on its own. Observations can be accommodated by adjusting other parts of the theory. This "holism" implies that scientific theories are underdetermined by evidence, meaning multiple theories can explain the same empirical data. In Quine's view, a good theory fits well with the entire web of scientific knowledge and can accommodate new observations within the existing structure.</p>


<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Bas van Fraassen (b. 1941) Constructive Empiricism</strong></h2>


<p>Van Fraassen, a leading figure in the philosophy of science, argued against the need for theories to be true in a literal sense. In his view, science aims to develop "empirically adequate" theories, meaning they successfully describe observable phenomena. His "constructive empiricism" suggests that as long as a theory makes correct predictions about observable facts, it is useful—even if it does not accurately describe unobservable entities (e.g., atoms, quarks). For van Fraassen, a good theory provides empirical adequacy—it works by predicting and explaining the observable world without claiming it is literally true.</p>


<h2 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Hilary Putnam (1926–2016). Realism and Pragmatism</strong></h2>


<p>Putnam engaged deeply with the question of scientific realism—whether scientific theories describe the world as it really is or are just useful tools. He argued for an "internal realism," in which scientific truths depend on our conceptual schemes and practices but still aim to reflect reality. He also emphasized the "pragmatic" criterion, meaning that theories should be judged by their usefulness and success in practical application. According to Putnam, a good theory works pragmatically, balancing both explanatory power and empirical success while acknowledging that our access to "truth" is mediated by human conceptual frameworks.</p>

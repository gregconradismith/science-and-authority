<p>In this course we identified the Scientific Revolution as being associated with the Enlightenment, Modernity, and figures such as Francis Bacon and Galileo Galilei.  The only serious attempt at demarcating science from non-science that we discussed was Karl Popper's view that scientific theories must be falsifiable.  We noted that Popper is one of the few philosophers of science who is universally recognized by professional scientists, most of whom have little interest in philosophy or the "demarcation problem."   </p>


<p>Falsifiability or testability of scientific hypotheses is important.  A case in point is recent discussion in the physics community as to whether string theory can be considered "science" given our inability to construct the instruments that would be required for experimental confirmation.  Perhaps string theory would be better described as beautiful mathematics that might one day become physics.  But falsifiability and testability are only one aspect of science as a social phenomenon. </p>


<p>Jonathan Marks begins his book <em>Why I Am Not a Scientist: Anthropology and Modern Knowledge</em> with this definition:  Science is the production of convincing knowledge in modern society.  This definition is generous.  Any good definition of science will be generous.  However, the phrase "convincing knowledge" bypasses several issues that we have emphasized in this course.  For example, if our epistemology (theory of knowledge, how we know what we know) includes non-scientific "ways of knowing" … are these to be considered "science" just because we are convinced?  [ Say historical research provides convincing evidence that George Washington owned a cat.  In what sense is <em>that</em> knowledge production scientific? ] </p>


<p>Most importantly, I do not want to beg the question: Why is scientific knowledge <em>not convincing</em> to the denier?  For this reason, I propose the following alternative (and generous) answer to the question <em>What is Science?</em></p>


<p class="has-vivid-red-color has-text-color"><strong>Science is the production of reliable knowledge in modern society - knowledge that is <span style="text-decoration:underline">reliable</span> precisely because it is <span style="text-decoration:underline">publicly validated</span></strong>.</p>


<p>This definition is similar in spirit to writings of both Jonathan Marks and Naomi Oreskes.  I find this particular definition helpful in understanding the complexities of scientific knowledge in relation to "other ways of knowing."  </p>


<p>What I am saying is that science - at its best - generates reliable knowledge in a particular way (perhaps not the only way).  Although - as we have discussed - scientific practices must be adjusted depending on the domain of application (e.g., natural sciences vs. human sciences), the scientific method - to the extent that there is one - involves production of knowledge that is "reliable" because it is "publicly validated." </p>


<p>The honest and rigorous attempt at validation is what makes scientific knowledge more than opinion or commentary.  The "public" aspect is required for any process of validation to be "convincing."  This public aspect of scientific knowledge is also what rules out arguments from authority, revelation, personal whimsy, and so on.  Such things are ruled out, not as <strong>metaphysical presupposition</strong>, but as <strong>a practical precondition to public validation</strong>.</p>


<p>Scientific knowledge is reliable whether or not everyone is convinced. </p>


<p>Public validation refers to the social process of vetting, i.e., the process of carefully and critically examining something (as in Oreskes).  </p>


<p>In the so-called experimental sciences, reproducibility is an obvious and important aspect of testing hypotheses. However, in some sciences public validation may proceed in spite of the fact that reproducibility is not possible (as is the case for some aspects of natural history, cosmology, etc.).  </p>


<p>My answer to the question <em>What is Science?</em> is that science is the production of reliable (publicly validated) knowledge.  This leads to questions that we may consider later:</p>


<ul>
<li>Do appropriate methods for vetting knowledge depend on the context of who is doing science (and for whom)? </li>
</ul>


<ul>
<li>Can the phenomenon of science denial be understood as the consequence of losing a "civic space" for public vetting of knowledge?  </li>
</ul>

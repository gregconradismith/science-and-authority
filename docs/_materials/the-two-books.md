<p>In <em>The New Atlantis</em>&nbsp;- the utopian novel by&nbsp;<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Bacon">Sir Francis Bacon</a>&nbsp;that envisions&nbsp;the modern research university&nbsp;- </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:quote -->
<blockquote class="wp-block-quote"><!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>... the English vessel sets sail from Peru westbound across the Pacific … [and comes] upon an island that was not on their charts….&nbsp;&nbsp;After landing and disembarking, the crew discovered that the community, called&nbsp;Bensalem, was ruled by an academy of scholars called&nbsp;Salomon’s House. Its leaders were priests, and they used science and technology to improve their lives. (Crease, p. 28) </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph --></blockquote>
<!-- /wp:quote -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In&nbsp;this book and others, Bacon argued that a King has a moral duty to promote the study and control of nature and to devote the power of his kingdom to seeking discoveries systematically.  (Crease, p. 33, paraphrased)</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:quote -->
<blockquote class="wp-block-quote"><!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>God created <strong>two books </strong>for humans, Bacon said, the<strong> Bible and Nature.</strong> In the first, we read of God’s will which includes the instruction, laid out in Genesis, that we humans rule over the fish in the sea, the birds in the heavens, and the Earth and everything on it.&nbsp;&nbsp;We can find the tools to do so, however, in the second book. </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph --></blockquote>
<!-- /wp:quote -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Today we will revisit the two-books idea beginning with Creases' comment that on p. 35 that the two-book idea marks the birth of the gap between the two academic cultures, namely, the humanities and the sciences.&nbsp;</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2>The Two Academic Cultures of C. P. Snow</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Crease suggests that the two-books idea prefigures the division of academic cultures: humanities (bible) and science (nature).  He is, of course, referring to the two academic cultures of C. P. Snow.  As described in Jonathan Marks' wonderful book <a rel="noreferrer noopener" href="https://www.amazon.com/Why-I-Am-Not-Scientist/dp/0520259602/" target="_blank">Why I Am Not a Scientist: Anthropology and Modern Knowledge</a> (2009), ...</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:quote -->
<blockquote class="wp-block-quote"><!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>C.P. Snow was a distinguished physicist at Cambridge as well as a successful novelist. Ever since his essay “The Two Cultures” appeared in the 1950s, the academy has been forced to acknowledge that [scientific knowledge has become] so specialized that a scientist often knows nothing but science. … Snow set out the proposition that academic life was increasingly becoming [split between humanities and sciences, even to] the point of becoming distinct campus cultures.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph --><cite>Jonathan Marks, Why I Am Not a Scientist, Ch. 1, Science as a Culture and as a "Side" (both here and below, edited down from pp. 6-10).</cite></blockquote>
<!-- /wp:quote -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>As STEM majors, I'm sure you have noticed that these two academic camps...</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:quote -->
<blockquote class="wp-block-quote"><!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>think about the world differently, have different interests, languages, value systems, and can hardly communicate meaningfully with one another.  </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In recent years, discussions about the science-humanities divide in academics and in life [continue] and [no one believes that] the situation is improving. On campus, humanists publish books, scientists publish articles. As a result, humanists tend to read books, and scientists tend to read articles. ... [Most academics] find it more than a full-time activity to keep up to date on our subspecialty (say, molecular anthropology), much less on our specialty (biological anthropology), much less on our general field (anthropology…), still less on other sciences—and still have the time and mental energy to read novels. </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>This “cultural” difference is manifested in other ways as well. In the late 1950s, the National Science Foundation was still relatively new, and cold war anxieties … were promoting both the expansion of science and the massive transfer of financial support for it from the shoulders of private foundations to the government bureaucracy. [Note from gdCS: Today NIH funding surpasses NSF and the private-to-public trend has reversed.] Not surprisingly, the academy began to change, as did its priorities. Major universities that had long been known as centers for training and research began to rely on research grants as a major source of their operating budget. This in turn placed a new emphasis on the ability of new faculty to apply successfully for grants to fund their research. Indeed, anything else they did began to be downplayed; a science department’s significance became measurable in terms of the amount of grant money it brought in. And if the quality of teaching, or even the structure of the curriculum, suffered thereby, it was just an unfortunate consequence of the ruthlessly competitive marketplace, in which scientific grants dictated both academic stature and clout within the university.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>It wasn’t at all the same in the humanities. In the humanities there was no money—or, at least, very little. Consequently, your advancement wasn’t predicated quite so much on getting grant moneys. If you did, that was a feather in your cap, but it wasn’t like biochemistry, where you were simply expected to. In both the sciences and the humanities you were expected to publish, but publication in the sciences required significantly more investment of capital. Consequently, faculty in the sciences were being recruited more and more one-dimensionally, as departments loaded up on faculty in hot (i.e., fundable) areas of research. If the curriculum suffered, so be it. </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>[At] Yale in the 1980s, the biology department had nobody who could teach undergraduate anatomy. Anatomy had to be taught in the anthropology department. Weirder still, students who took, say, Comparative Primate Anatomy did not get science credit for it. They got social science credit, since the home department, anthropology, was officially a social science department. It didn’t matter that the course was, by anyone’s definition, science; that it was a laboratory class; that it was competently taught; and that it was filling a significant gap in the curriculum. What mattered was that we needed to patrol the boundaries of science—particularly in its bureaucratized form—and aggressively regulate it. … [T]he ability to decide what counted as science [at Yale] was a form of social power that was not to be surrendered lightly.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>...  [In 1995] a mathematician and a biologist published <em>Higher Superstition: The Academic Left and Its Quarrels with Science</em>. This book represented one front of what came to be known as the <strong>“science wars” </strong>in the mid-1990s and suggested that there was some sort of anti-science conspiracy on the part of creationists, animal rights activists, philosophers and historians of science, and literary critics—in short, on the part of seemingly anyone who had anything remotely critical to say about any aspect of science.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>But what struck me most was a thought experiment the authors suggested. If the humanities faculty of a university <code><em>were to walk out in a huff, the scientific faculty could, at need and with enough released time, patch together a humanities curriculum, to be taught by scientists themselves. It would have obvious gaps and rough spots to be sure, and it might with some regularity prove inane; but on the whole it would be, we imagine, no worse than operative. What the opposite situation—a walkout by the scientists—would produce, as the humanities department tried to cope with the demand for science education, we leave to the reader’s imagination.</em></code> To my imagination, at least, the result would probably be a science curriculum with “gaps and rough spots” as well, but one on the whole far better taught than previously.  </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph --><cite>Jonathan Marks, Why I Am Not a Scientist, Ch. 1, Science as a Culture and as a "Side" (both here and below, edited down from pp. 6-10).</cite></blockquote>
<!-- /wp:quote -->

<!-- wp:heading -->
<h2>The <em>Fashionable Nonsense</em> story</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:quote -->
<blockquote class="wp-block-quote"><!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><em>Fashionable Nonsense: Postmodern Intellectuals' Abuse of Science</em> (1998) is a book by physicists <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Sokal">Alan Sokal</a> and <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Bricmont">Jean Bricmont</a>. As part of the so-called <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_wars">science wars</a>, Sokal and Bricmont criticize <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodernism">postmodernism</a> in academia for the misuse of scientific and mathematical concepts in postmodern writing.  According to some reports, the response within the humanities was "polarized"; critics of Sokal and Bricmont charged that they lacked understanding of the writing they were scrutinizing. By contrast, responses from the scientific community were more supportive.  (Wikipedia)</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Sokal is best known for his eponymous <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sokal_affair">1996 hoaxing affair</a>, whereby he was able to get published a deliberately absurd article that he submitted to <em><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Text">Social Text</a></em>, a <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_theory">critical theory</a> journal.  The article itself is included in <em>Fashionable Nonsense</em> as an appendix.  (Wikipedia)</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph --></blockquote>
<!-- /wp:quote -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>I highly recommend Jonathan Marks' description (pp. 10-13) of the Sokal hoax.  Think about the reaction of prominent scientists (who were quite amused) and what this implies regarding the two academic cultures. </p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading">The Funding Effect</h3>


<blockquote class="wp-block-quote">
<p>The manipulation and selective publication of data by Big Tobacco’s scientists resulted in a distortion of the literature now widely known as the ‘‘funding effect,’’ a term used to describe<span style="text-decoration:underline"> the close correlation between the results desired by a study’s sponsors and the results reported</span>.  (Michaels, p. 86)</p>
</blockquote>


<blockquote class="wp-block-quote">
<p>... However, skewed literature reviews would never be enough to win the day, certainly not when the issue is cigarette smoke. The results of some studies are simply too powerful to explain away with sleight of hand; they must be attacked head on. This is where <span style="text-decoration:underline">data reanalysis</span> comes in. In all valid studies, the methods must be selected before any data are analyzed; post hoc reanalyses are clearly susceptible to mercenary manipulation since after-the-fact researchers can change the parameters of the study to yield the preferred new outcome. (Michaels, p. 86)</p>
</blockquote>

<blockquote class="wp-block-quote">
<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Regulation and Defense of Secondhand Smoke is Ongoing </h3>


<p>Where does everything stand today? [2008] The Federal Trade Commission has mandated advertising restrictions, the Federal Aviation Agency has outlawed smoking on commercial airliners, and federal contracts now require a smoke-free workplace, a strong incentive for many employers to ban workplace smoking. Cigarettes are taxed at the federal and state level—a type of regulation (and the main one, in the case of smoking, according to some observers). Otherwise, OSHA has backed off, the EPA has backed off, and Congress has yet to give the FDA jurisdiction over cigarettes. The most important changes in the regulation of tobacco have occurred in the states and localities, many of which have indeed passed restrictions on indoor smoking that leave people standing in the rain outside office buildings smoking their cigarettes. After decreasing for eight years, the percentage of American adults who smoke appears to have leveled at about 21 percent (<a rel="noreferrer noopener" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17065979/" target="_blank">MMWR Morb Mortal Wkly Rep 2006</a>); cigarettes are still responsible for more than four hundred thousand deaths each year and cost approximately $157 billion in annual health-related economic losses (<a rel="noreferrer noopener" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20669512/" target="_blank">The Health Consequences of Smoking: A Report of the Surgeon General, 2004</a>). In some parts of the country, like California, the prevalence of smoking continues to decrease, which suggests that well-funded antismoking programs can further reduce smoking rates and save more lives. (p. 90)</p>
</blockquote>

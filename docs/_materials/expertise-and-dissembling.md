<h3>My Engineer Friend Has Doubts</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>One of my friends is a professor of engineering.  Engineers of her caliber understand that physicists estimate the age of the universe to be about 14 billion years and geologists estimate that the earth is about 4.5 billion years old. She takes these estimates at face value.  </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Today, I learned that my engineer colleague is skeptical about evolution.&nbsp; To me, the scientific consensus on issues such as descent with modification and a long history of life on earth are among the most well-documented of historical facts. To me, working out the details of the "pattern and process" of evolution and evolutionary genetic mechanisms are among the most interesting areas of contemporary research.&nbsp;</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>My question for you is this: How should I interpret my colleague's doubts?&nbsp; She's not stupid, but she is wrong on this one.&nbsp;&nbsp;And just to make things more challenging, please understand that - in this parable - my engineer colleague and I have very similar religious beliefs.  That is, your first hypothesis - that she is a creationist and I am not - this is <strong>not</strong> the explanation for our <strong>different levels of skepticism</strong> regarding biological evolution.  In that case, <strong>what accounts for the difference</strong>?</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:separator -->
<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity" />

<!-- wp:heading {"level":3} -->
<h3>Denying Climate Science</h3>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Now consider the contemporary socio-political problem of "science denial."&nbsp;&nbsp; Why is the American public not convinced about the reality (and peril) of declining biodiversity, human-induced climate change, and so on? The vast majority of professional scientists are convinced.&nbsp; One might presume that the problem is the <strong>lack of public understanding of science</strong>. </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Perhaps, in a democracy, the political will to address climate change can only arise in the context of a public that has intellectual mastery of climate science.&nbsp;&nbsp; Perhaps.  But - I think you will agree - that is only one way of framing the science denial problem.  </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Consider the two preliminary thoughts that follow.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:separator -->
<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity" />
<!-- /wp:separator -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>(1) My personal experience talking to evolution-skeptical physicists and engineers suggests that biological scientists "think differently" that physical scientists.  This makes me wonder if the <strong>typical non-scientist "thinks differently" than the typical scientist</strong>. </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Perhaps, in a general sense, <strong>laymen think differently than experts</strong>.&nbsp; Even when adequately/equally informed about facts. Even after controlling for factors such as raw intelligence and curiosity. If this is true,  then the&nbsp;solution to the climate change denial problem might be at the finger tips of a particular type of expert - a person who knows (or at least knows how to study) how the evaluation of evidence depends on a person's status as layman or expert.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>For Your Notebook:</strong> Imagine you wanted to talk to a fellow student who might know about this expert/layman issue.  In order to do this, you decide to post to a message board for W&amp;M students majoring in X.  What is the best choice for X?&nbsp;</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:separator -->
<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity" />
<!-- /wp:separator -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>(2) Did you know that publicly traded energy companies (ExxonMobil, Royal Dutch Shell, Chevron) are corporations and that, under US law, <strong>corporations are `persons'</strong>?   Business executives of corporations have a `fiduciary responsibility' to make decisions that maximizes profit for stock-holders, even if such action is unethical and obviously not in the interest of `stakeholders' or the broader public.&nbsp;</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>It is not uncommon, and not illegal, for a corporation to fund individuals and institutions to perform intellectual work (writing essays, doing research, giving speeches) that is designed to confuse the media landscape and academic press on issues related to energy and climate change.&nbsp;  </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>How's that for a 'funding effect'?</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Perhaps the climate science denial problem boils down to the number of scientists, lawyers, and business leaders who are willing to make a living through the intellectually dishonest practice of <a href="../merchants-of-doubt-2/">manufacturing doubt</a> through disinformation.&nbsp;  </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p><strong>For Your Notebook:</strong> If you write and publish an article in the Flat Hat that promulgates disinformation (falsehoods) to change student opinion on an issue, have you violated W&amp;M's honor code?</p>

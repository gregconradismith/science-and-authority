<p>This unit of <em>Science &amp; Authority</em> has focused on several "episodes" or “case studies.”  In the first two - <em>Diagnosis: Mercury</em> and <em>An Enemy of the People</em> - the scientist is protagonist, a discovery is made that affects public health, there is trouble getting the word out, and there is resistance by the individuals or industries who see their interests as adversely affected by the proposed remedies (lowering of official levels of exposures allowed, the cost of replacing and re-engineering the city water supply).  </p>


<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Diagnosis: Mercury and An Enemy of the People </h2>


<p>Dr. Hightower is accused of being “an activist” by people with a vested interest in minimizing the regulation of environmental mercury. But, at least in the narrative, her concern over the welfare of her patients is genuine. She is fair-minded about the question of what mercury exposure levels should be tolerated.  Her primary concern is her patients; secondarily, she has become concerned about clarity and transparency in the regulation of methylmercury exposure.</p>


<p><em>Diagnosis: Mercury</em> leaves us with ethical concerns - or, at least, leaves us in a state of quandary - regarding how to interpret the behavior of the Food &amp; Drug Administration.  The FDA is a government agency whose <a href="https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/what-we-do" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">mission</a> is “to protect the public health by ensuring the safety, efficacy, and security of human and veterinary drugs, biological products, and medical devices; and by ensuring the safety of our nation's food supply, cosmetics, and products that emit radiation.” However, when protecting public health involves regulating industry (e.g., the Commercial Fishing Industry), Hightower's narrative suggests that the public interest is not the FDA's only concern.  </p>


<p>In <em>Making Sense of Science</em> by Cornelia Dean, the chapter titled <em>The Universal Solvent</em> is about science and science funding (i.e., money is the univeral solvent). The second half of the chapter focuses on medicine and human nutrition and the Food and Drug Administration does not fare very well in that context (e.g., the food pyramid). I do not have a deep understanding of regulatory bodies such as the Food and Drug Administration, but I do know that the production and dissemination of accurate, science-based information that the public needs to make decisions about a healthy diet – this basic function of the FDA - has been criticized (Michael Moss, 2013. Salt Sugar Fat: How the Food Giants Hooked Us <a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/1400069807)">Amazon</a>. But, in defense of the FDA, as Michaels points out in his discussion of OSHA, federal agencies must continually make practical decisions about what can be accomplished with limited resources, and without taking actions that generate political blowback (e.g., from the agribusiness lobby).</p>


<p>We next discussed <em>An Enemy of the People</em>, a story that is similar in some ways to <em>Diagnosis: Mercury</em>. Some have suggested that there is room for a critical take on the actions and character of the scientist protagonist Dr. Stockmann.  Certainly, in Arthur Miller's adaptation, Stockmann expresses an anti-democratic interpretation of his predicament (and even more so in Ibsen's original version of the play).</p>


<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Beryllium and Secondhand Smoke </h2>


<p>Another case study was workplace exposure to the carcinogen Beryllium – "a chemical element with the symbol "Be" and atomic number 4. It is a steel-gray, strong, lightweight and brittle alkaline earth metal" (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beryllium" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Wikipedia</a>). Beryllium is technologically important because "it has a large scattering cross section for high-energy neutrons". Beryllium is a `neutron moderator' used in nuclear reactors. In some nuclear weapons, thin beryllium foil is "the very outer layer of the plutonium pits whose implosion occurs during the primary stages of thermonuclear bombs."  Thus, Beryllium is of great import to the weapons industry and the Department of Defense as well as the Department of Energy.  </p>


<p>In David Michael's narratives about Beryllium and secondhand smoke, scientists are both protagonists and antagonists.  Scientific questions and political realities overlap in a way that renders the science untrustworthy.  You might be left wondering: Can scientists in these industries be expected – without coercion of government agencies – to properly balance safety against other concerns that affect their industry and livelihoods?  </p>


<p>The beryllium and secondhand smoke readings show how scientists are situated throughout industry and government as well as academia.  These different types of professionals might share technical expertise, but - I wonder - is there really a "scientific ethos" (ala Merton) that encapsulates and transcends the different concerns, values, and standard modes of operation of scientists in these different societal spheres?</p>


<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The <strong>Funding Effect</strong></h2>


<p>There are strong indications in our case studies that scientific recommendations are succeptible to the interests of the organizations in which scientists work.  Barnes &amp; Bero (1998) <a href="https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.279.19.1566">[DOI]</a> analyzed review articles on the health effects of passive smoking (secondhand smoke). They show that tobacco-affiliated scientists reach different conclusions than scientists who are not affiliated with this industry.  This so-called "funding effect" is one reason why scientific journals often require authors to declare their sources of funding, and to report any associations that might be interpreted by others as a conflicts of interest. </p>


<p>The “funding effect” does not necessarily indicate fraudulent activity. More likely, this strong correlation between the position of scientists and the conclusion of their studies reveals a selection bias. For example, perhaps scientists who choose to work for tobacco have certain predispositions that influence the type of studies with which they participate. </p>


<p>There is also the issue that studies design and statistical analysis differs (and are rightly so) depending on the specific aims of the study, which are usually more precisely formulated than "passive smoking is harmful" or "passive smoking is not harmful."  When questions are posed so simply, they may in fact be ill-posed, from a strictly technical perspective.  Are we talking about cancer or asthma?  Indoors or outdoors?  Etc.</p>


<p>The broader implications of the "funding effect" are perhaps of more concern.  Public and private institutions in our society employ scientists who are funded to work on projects of various kinds.  What is learned by their research - the knowledge that is purchased - depends very much on what questions are asked.  Perhaps some questions just aren't that interesting to industry because there is no conceivable way that this information could be "monetized" (to generate profit).  </p>


<p>Perhaps, then, it is fortunate that there are many academic scientists in America’s research universities who are focused on basic/pure scientific exploration and (some of them) are not tied so directly to short term economic gain.  But perhaps these scientists are driven by "scientific merit" - what is intellectually very interesting - more so than what is of practical benefit to society.  If so, who is going to research the mundane and boring scientific questions whose answers might be of great important and possible benefit? It seems to me that the questions of "regulatory science" and "environmental toxicity" can be unglamorous and unprofitable to individual careers, and this may be part of the problem.</p>


<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The Burden of Regulation </h2>


<p>The cases we have studied do not address the issue of "regulatory overburden." If the beryllium standard is too restrictive, then nuclear weapons cost more. These higher costs will be passed on to the public, not absorbed by defense contractors.  If OSHA sets a standard that is too permissive - or sets no standard - then cases of beryllium-induced cancer go up and, in addition to the human suffering involved, the costs of medical insurance, medical treatment, and litigation will be passed on to the taxpaying public.  So maybe this type of regulatory question is more of a balancing act than idealists  might first suppose.   Maybe we should not be too quick to judge. </p>


<p>Allow me to conclude with four take home messages.</p>


<p>(1) Industry scientists are allowed to have interests and viewpoints.  But given the “funding effect,” the public trust of scientists and the results of scientific research absolutely requires full disclosure of conflicts of interest.</p>


<p>(2) The deliberate manufacturing of doubt – designing research studies whose purpose is not clarification of an issue, but simply to confuse the issue – is well document in the operations of big tobacco and beyond.  I would argue that “manufacturing uncertainty” is unethical behavior, pure and simple.  “There are rules, even in war.”  If over-regulation is a concern, industry can make that point by explicitly including economic interests in their analyses of risks to public health. </p>


<p>Scientists themselves must ensure this behavior is not rewarded, in the same way that lawyers and doctors discipline themselves.  When it comes to academic science as practiced at research universities, there are efforts to censure scientists who falsify data, fail to report conflicts of interest, and operate in other unseemly ways.  This occurs at the level of funding agencies, the research offices of universities, and as part of peer review and publication.  I would be interested in learning more about practices of self-regulation among communities of government and industrial scientists. </p>


<p>(3) I admit that markets can be good and regulation can be burdensome.  But it is important to distinguish between regulations that limit personal liberties, or make it more difficult for a small business to thrive, from the "overburden" – read “decreased profit” – that is of concern to corporations.  Pollution is an “externality” that must be regulated by government.  Industry is essential, but those making bank from industrial activities ought to pay the full cost of doing business.  Environmental toxicity is a tremendous problem that rises to the crisis level - along with other pressing issues such as extinction/biodiversity, climate change, and emerging diseases.  It is essential that government agencies with mandates to protect public health are allowed to do so without undue interference from private interests.</p>


<p>(4) As Heather Douglas notes, debates in the regulatory "arena center on how much evidence is sufficient or when a particular study is sufficiently reliable. The arguments focus on such questions as: How much of an understanding of biochemical mechanisms do we need to have before we regulate a chemical? How much evidence of causation is needed before a court case should be won? How much of an understanding of complex biological or geological systems do we need before regulatory frameworks intervene in the market to prevent potential harm?  The idea that science is the authoritative body to which one should turn is not questioned; what is questioned is which science is adequate for the job, or which scientific experts are to be believed by policymakers, Congress, and the public.” (p. 5) </p>


<p>Provided all parties operate with scientific integrity, such questions are quite reasonable.  Deciding which risks are acceptable and what to do about them is a deeply political process (political in the positive sense we have discussed).  “This process should not be confused with assessing the nature and extent of risks. On the one hand, the acceptability of risk is an ethical, social, and ultimately a democratic issue, rather than a scientific one. Simply because a risk is assessed does not mean we must act to mitigate it. On the other hand, we do not want to succumb to the temptation of wishing risks away that might lead to uncomfortable political problems.” (p. 141)  </p>


<p>Scientific integrity is a rightful concern, but - even so - we must not lose sight of “the proper locus for decision making authority in the public realm.” (p. 134).  “…[T]he ultimate source of political authority for any democratic government is the citizenry. In the United States, the agencies that generate regulations do so by virtue of legislative authority from Congress, and are accountable to Congress for their regulatory decisions. Congress in turn represents the people of the United States, who hold their representatives to Congress to account on election day for their legislative actions (at least in the ideal). The agencies that write regulatory rules do so to implement the laws crafted by Congress. Thus, the federal agencies are legally responsible for final decisions (“rule-makings”) at the regulatory level, and agencies are held accountable by Congress through public hearings and budget appropriations (Fiorino 1995, 67–70).... The holding of decision making authority by the appointed official means that the<strong> scientific input can be only advice</strong>.”  </p>


<p>Acknowledging this - that scientific input on regulatory matters can only be advice - brings us back to politics, competing values, civic virtues, and the necessity of moral reasoning.  Giambattista Vico – there is a chapter on him in Creases' <em>The Worshop and the World</em> – believed that modern education pays “an excessive amount of attention to the natural sciences and not enough to ethics,” the Liberal Arts &amp; Sciences, and the Humanities.  Given the history and philosophy that infuses our Science &amp; Authority readings, you will, by the end of this semester, know for certain whether you agree with Vico on this point!</p>

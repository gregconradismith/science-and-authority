<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Overview</h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Falsification (the idea that scientific theories must be testable) has been proposed as the principle that marks the boundary between science and non-science. If only it were that simple.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Prepare to Discuss </h2>
<!-- /wp:heading -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Consider what philosopher Robert P. Crease had to say on the centenary of the birth of Karl Popper.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:quote -->
<blockquote class="wp-block-quote"><!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>As a philosopher of science, I should honour my field’s heroes, especially those recognized by outsiders. I should, in particular, cherish those whose doctrines are understood and valued by scientists themselves. Thus, 2002 should have found me busy celebrating the centenary of the birth of Karl Popper. However, I was unenthusiastic.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Popper, who died in 1994, is hailed on one Web page as “the most important philosopher of science since Francis Bacon” (1561-1626).&nbsp;...  <strong>Popper is also the only philosopher of science who is a household name among scientists</strong>.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Most can state the essentials of his chief teaching the “<strong>principle of falsifiability</strong>”. This is the idea that science progresses by “conjectures and refutations” and not (as Bacon suggested) through “<strong>inductive generalizations</strong>”, in which one generalizes on the basis of a sample. Popper, in other words, thought that a theory cannot be proved right, only wrong. A theory becomes scientific by exposing itself to the possibility of being proved incorrect.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>This principle seems to solve at a stroke what philosophers of science call the <strong>problem of demarcation</strong>, or how to draw the line between science and non-science. “The criterion of the scientific status of a theory is its falsifiability, or refutability, or testability,” wrote Popper in&nbsp;<em>Conjectures and Refutations&nbsp;</em>in 1963. Einstein’s relativity theory, for example, is scientific, while astrology, Marxism, psychoanalysis and new-age systems are not. <em><strong>The problem with such dogmas and ideologies, for Popperians, is not that they cannot be confirmed, but that they find confirmation everywhere.</strong></em> They are unscientific because they do not offer themselves up to potential refutation.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph --></blockquote>
<!-- /wp:quote -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Crease 2002 (see Further Reading).  </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<p>A professor says "There is no <strong>scientific</strong> controversy about [TOPIC]; there is a <strong>sociopolitical</strong> controversy about [TOPIC]." To what [TOPIC] might she be referring?  Is it ever really that simple?</p>


<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Overview of <em>Science &amp; Authority</em> </h2>


<p>Modern science stands as a powerful cultural authority, influencing our world for better and for worse. But what legitimizes science as an authority? Are its foundational assumptions truly self-evident? How should we, as scientists, confront the rising tide of science denial? While we recognize and value the immense benefits that science has brought to society, how do we resist the dehumanizing aspects of an increasingly technological world? And in our fast-paced, pluralistic, post-fact society, what is the status of traditional scientific values like honesty, communalism, and disinterested research?</p>


<p>This course will tackle these challenging questions through a series of case studies, examining instances where science has gone astray—both historically, such as with tobacco, eugenics, and scientific racism, and in contemporary contexts, like propaganda, surveillance, fraud, and dead-end research. We will critically engage with postmodern critiques of science, questioning the intellectual hegemony that often frames scientific ‘objectivity’ as a tool for justifying dominant cultures and preserving the status quo.</p>


<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity" />


<p>I once heard someone complain, “I went to college hoping to <strong>learn how to think</strong>. Instead, I was <strong>taught what to think</strong>.” Putting the politics <a href="../learning-how-to-think-about-science/">Page</a> of the comment aside, the options for <strong>how to think about science and society</strong> will be a theme of this course.</p>


<p>In a typical skillfully taught science course, students are presented with a high-quality exposition of contemporary knowledge. Modern science is awe inspiring, but sometimes such classes are little more than an information download.  Usually the focus is on what to think—delivering the current scientific consensus in fields like molecular biology, chemistry, or neuroscience. Rarely do STEM students—or even professors—have the opportunity to explore how to think about science, its role in society, and the broader meaning it may hold. This course will offer you that opportunity, encouraging you to reflect on the bigger picture.</p>


<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity" />


<p>In science education there is an emphasis on the <strong>positive <a href="https://www.khanacademy.org/economics-finance-domain/ap-macroeconomics/basic-economics-concepts-macro/introduction-to-the-economic-way-of-thinking-macro/v/normative-and-positive-statements">Publisher</a></strong> (a philosophical term for statements about <strong>what is</strong>) and an unease with the <strong><em>normative <a href="https://www.khanacademy.org/economics-finance-domain/ap-macroeconomics/basic-economics-concepts-macro/introduction-to-the-economic-way-of-thinking-macro/v/normative-and-positive-statements">Publisher</a></em></strong> (statements about <strong>what ought to be</strong>).1 One reason for this is that professors feel an obligation to pass on what we already know (the scientific certitudes mentioned above). The amount of information relevant to students of science is enormous and always growing. There is no time to explore the bigger picture.</p>


<p>Another reason is that science itself does not address questions of meaning, including the meaning of science. Max Weber <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Weber">Publisher</a> (1864--1920), a key figure in the development of modern social sciences, once wrote: “What should we do? How should we live? The fact that science does not give us the answer to these questions is completely undeniable.”2</p>


<p>If science does not provide answers to questions of meaning or <em>what ought to be</em>, where should we seek them? In the humanities <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanities">Publisher</a> and high culture <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_culture">Publisher</a>? Through our political and religious commitments? We will explore these questions throughout the course. For now, let us simply acknowledge that a science professor does not necessarily possess more expertise or wisdom about the bigger picture than anyone else—which may explain why these broader questions are seldom addressed in science lectures.</p>


<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity" />


<blockquote class="wp-block-quote has-text-align-left">
<p>Who still believes today that a knowledge of astronomy or biology or physics or chemistry could teach us anything at all about the meaning of the world? </p>
<cite>Max Weber, Science as a Vocation</cite></blockquote>


<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity" />


<p>What resources do we have, as students and scientists, to help us think about science? Max Weber suggests that the <em>scientific frame of mind</em> – whatever that means – might help answer questions of meaning, provided we pose the questions correctly. This brings us to another theme of this course - the question of whether the way that professional scientists think is somehow different from thinking in the manner of a professional sociologist or philosopher. If so, perhaps <em>learning how to think about science and society in these various ways</em> will help your generation find answers to the vexing science &amp; society questions of the day, such as alternative facts, science denial, propaganda, surveillance, and ecological collapse.</p>


<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity" />


<p>One criterion for being enrolled in this course is that you are a student of the natural world and quantitative reasoning. Whether or not you consider yourself a scientist in training, you are majoring in what William &amp; Mary’s COLL curriculum describes as an NQR discipline. Your professors have organized themselves into three broad academic categories (knowledge domains, ways of knowing):</p>


<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Natural World and Quantitative Reasoning (NQR) </li>


<li>Culture, Society, and the Individual (CSI) </li>


<li>Arts, Letter, and Values (ALV)</li>
</ul>


<p>In my personal experience, scientists, engineers, and mathematicians (NQR-types) tend to be naive concerning sociology (CSI) and philosophy (ALV) of science when compared, say, to professors of the humanities or a well-read citizen scholar. This is one reason why we will begin <em>Science & Authority</em> by reading sociology, philosophy, and literature, beginning with Richard Sennett and Isaac Ariail Reed (see the next page titled <strong><em>What is Authority? <a href="../what-is-authority/)</em></strong>">Page</a>.</p>

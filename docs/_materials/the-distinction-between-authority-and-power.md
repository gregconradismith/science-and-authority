<h2><em>What Is Authority?</em> - Lecture 1 - The distinction between authority and power </h2>


<h2><strong><a href="https://isaacreed.net/about-and-contact/">Isaac Ariail Reed</a></strong></h2>


<figure class="wp-block-audio aligncenter"><audio controls src="https://scienceauthority.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/lecture-1-introduction-and-the-distinction-between-power-and-authority-online-audio-converter.com_.mp3"></audio></figure>


<p>What is authority?<strong> </strong>We will begin with some basic distinctions and definitions from which your knowledge of this topic can grow. Of course, these are not intended to be the final word, but rather, the beginning of a semester long journey.</p>


<p>Let's begin with associations. When we say the word “authority,” or some of its sister words like “the authorities” or “authoritative,” what do we mean? I think we refer to a few different ideas. First, we often have some sense that we are referring to a hierarchy. We refer to the authorities as persons or groups somehow positioned higher than us. Those higher-up persons are there because of what they can do or say. We might say a person “has authority” when we think that they can be trusted. For example, when we take the advice of health professionals as to how to act during a pandemic, we are recognizing that they are positioned to give us direction, they are higher status than us in the health professional hierarchy. But we also take their advice because we trust that such officials are authoritative. That is, they are there, we think, for a good reason. They know something we don't, or they understand, better that we do, the “pandemic” situation. </p>


<p>It's worth pausing here for a moment to note two social institutions, of which you have firsthand experience, where we find authority the sense of both <strong>hierarchy</strong> and <strong>trust</strong>. These institutions also perhaps exemplify how skepticism towards authority can develop in these institutions.  There is a presumption that the person who is high status somehow knows better. </p>


<p>The first institution to consider is the <strong>family</strong>. That parents have something like authority over their children has been true in societies that in all other respects are vastly different from each other. Throughout human history there have been many kinds of societies structured in many ways, but we find again and again that in each society there is something like <strong>parental authority</strong>. Now as children themselves know very well, parental authority is far from perfect. But if there has ever been a moment when you did what your mother told you to without arguing it out, and without hearing all of her reasons, and without being threatened with punishment, but rather just because you presumed that she must know what is best for you, then you have had some experience of authority.</p>


<p>The second institution I want you to consider is <strong>higher education</strong> itself. In the space between professor and students, there is a presumption that the professor actually knows something [about authority or  science, for example]. Perhaps an even more important assumption is that the professor knows something about what you ought to know. There are some visible credentials that attest to my authority in this case. I have a Ph.D. I have published papers and peer-reviewed journals. There may also be ways for you yourself to go and check to see if I really do know what I am talking about. But this is tricky business. Part of what happens in college is that you learn to simultaneously trust and argue with your professors, and this can manifest itself in a kind of paradox. At university you learn how to think for yourself. How to judge a good truth claim from a bad one. How to trust some authorities and not others. But to learn how to think for yourself, you must trust that some of your professors, some of the time, know better than you. That is, you must accept some authority in order to learn to question authority. </p>


<hr class="wp-block-separator" />


<p>In discussing the institution of the family, we noted that the experience of parental authority was one where a child does what he is told without the threat of punishment. This brings us to the purpose of this lecture, which is to draw a <strong>preliminary distinction between authority and power</strong>. Sometimes in everyday speech we use these terms authority and power as synonyms for each other. But in this course we will try our best to think about authority and power as distinct, even if in the real world they tend to get conflated (i.e., mixed up with each other).</p>


<p>So how can we draw a distinction between power and authority? Let’s define terms as follows.  Power is the capacity or ability to impose one's will over and against the resistance of others. A power relationship is one in which one person or group can have their way over and against the desires or interests of another person or group. In contrast, authority relationships involve standards of action and belief that are internalized by the persons lower in the hierarchy (e.g., the persons who follow the suggestions of health professionals). Authority is the intangible quality possessed by those who give advice that is followed without struggle. </p>


<p>This distinction between power and authority introduces the possibility that someone who is more powerful than someone else nonetheless could take advice from, or seed authority to, the less powerful person. Still, many people high up in various social structures and organizations access both power and authority. The president of our university is probably a good example of this. For some things, some of the time, some of us would be quick to defer to his authority. Yet simultaneously we probably follow his directions at least in part because he has some power over us. Things get even more complicated when we distinguish between the specific flesh and blood person who is president of the university, Jim Ryan, and the position of the President of the University of Virginia, which many different individuals have occupied. We might ask: Does authority accrue to the person or to the position? What about power? We may come back to this difficult problem later. For now, let us focus on this preliminary distinction between power and authority. It is a very ancient distinction, some version of which has probably existed in every society in human history. </p>


<hr class="wp-block-separator" />


<p>An intuitive example of the difference between power and authority is the following.  When we say that a piece of writing, a novel or a journal article, is powerful we sometimes also say that it `moves’ us. By this we mean that we are kind of pushed and pulled by the author's words on the page. But if we say that a piece of writing is authoritative, we usually mean something quite different. For example, if we said that a biography of Isaac Newton is authoritative, we usually mean that its author has checked all of her sources, has read all the other biographies of Newton, and considered what they were right about and what they were wrong about, and has thus produced a narrative of Newton's life that can be relied upon for its truth. We might say that a biography is authoritative even if the writing did not move us.  And in saying that the biography was authoritative, we would be ascribing a different kind of status to the author than if we said that her writing was powerful.</p>


<p>At this point it will be useful to introduce one of the readings for this week: a small excerpt of Richard Sennett's book “Authority.” Richard Sennett was a sociologist and a public intellectual, perhaps most well-known for his book co-authored with Jonathan Cobb, “The Hidden Injuries of Class.” This book examined class difference in America and its impact on emotions and people's sense of self. In his book “Authority,” Sennett is interested some of the questions that will preoccupy us in this class.  He asks, “What is authority and where does it come from?”  </p>


<p>Sennett begins his book “Authority” with a concrete example. He contrasts two orchestra conductors: Pierre Monteaux and Auturo Toscanini. Toscanini, he says, created discipline in his orchestra by inspiring terror.  “He screamed, stamped his foot, even threw his stick at the players.” In contrast to this, Sennett explains Monteaux had “easy assurance which allowed him to exercise effective discipline over the players.” Monteaux would cue sections of the orchestra with only a slight movement of his eyebrow or hand. What is so important about this example, Sennett says, is that Monteaux is interpreted by his players in a particular way. “With Monteaux there was no coercion, no threat, there was simply a man who was trying to help one be better. Better, that is to say, play what he wanted because he knew his aura was one was of one who had achieved an understanding that made it possible for him to judge in the most relaxed way.”  In the contrast between Pierre Monteaux and Auturo Toscanini, we have a concrete representation of the difference between authority and power. We might say that the relationship between Toscanini and his orchestra was a power relationship, while the relationship between Monteaux and his orchestra was an authority relationship, because the Monteaux orchestra acts not out of fear of being yelled at, fired, or demoted, but rather out of fear of disappointing their leader whose judgment they trust. Perhaps this is the essence of an authority relationship. </p>


<p>For Sennett the concept of authority involves several aspects.  The first of these is the idea of judgment. Exercising good judgment is something that we all appreciate, but it is a famously difficult thing to talk about clearly. Exercising good judgment is somehow more specific than just following a well-known rule, and yet it is not entirely specific to a given situation or decision. We expect people with more experience to exercise better judgment because they have been in situations that are hard to judge before. And we also expect a given judgment to be sensitive to the context of the situation. For example, the orchestra conductor expects different things from different players depending on their idiosyncratic strengths and weaknesses, what is going on that day, their recent history, and so on. Sennett tells us that the way in which the players in the orchestra think Monteaux has excellent judgment is essential to his authority over the orchestra.  A little later on the same page, Sennett tells us that Monteaux was authoritative because he held his players to a certain very high standard.  The implication is that a high standard is something the players share with their conductor, that is, there is something important shared between those engaged in an authority relationship even if that relationship is hierarchical. </p>


<p>Finally, Sennett is very interested in the relaxed confidence of the conductor, which suggests that authority works precisely because those who are authoritative do not give off signs of nervousness or precarity.  Authoritative figures are not fragile or agitated. The authoritative person is (or appears to be) secure in the fact that they know better. An important question related to this interactional aspect of authority is whether this impression of being authoritative, relaxed and secure can be faked? Of course, being relaxed and secure may not be a property of all kinds of authority. For example, it may be that the author of a new biography of Isaac Newton is an entirely unrelaxed in person, and indeed a terrible interview.  Or perhaps a professor is not a very good lecturer, but we might still trust that professor as authoritative for other reasons.</p>


<hr class="wp-block-separator" />


<p>I would like to draw your attention to one other important part of Sennett's text on authority. He writes at one point that “he has never known a bad or an inept musician who managed to preserve authority over an orchestra for long.” Some strong musicians still don't manage to become good conductors, but there are no conductors who are weak musicians. In the elevated realm of art, apparently, being a virtuoso painter or musician seems pretty much like a necessary precondition for having authority. However, Sennett says in many other realms of life a different version of strength is required. In some realms of life being a virtuoso of sorts may not be necessary to come to the top of a hierarchy. Rather, a different kind of strength, one that he calls domination, can substitute.  Here again you see the contrast between authority - in this case, authoritative musical knowledge - to power, in the sense of direct and perhaps pernicious control over other people.  Sennett seems to be drawing a distinction between the realm of art and the realm of politics, insisting that in the realm of art there may exist a form of pure authority.  I'm not sure I agree with this, but it certainly poses an interesting question for us to consider.</p>

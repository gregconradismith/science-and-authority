The Grand Inquisitor says that humans crave security and certainty more than truth and freedom

I am not sure I did a great job explaining the relevance of the "The Grand Inquisitor” to our course. Allow me to try again.

“The Grand Inquisitor” is a story told within The Brothers Karamazov by Fyodor Dostoevsky. In the novel, the skeptical brother Ivan tells this story to his younger brother Alyosha, who is a novice monk. The setting is sixteenth-century Seville, at the height of the Spanish Inquisition. Christ returns to earth silently and begins performing miracles. The people recognize him. They are drawn to him immediately. But the Grand Inquisitor, a powerful cardinal, has Christ arrested.

That night, the Inquisitor visits Christ in his cell and delivers a long monologue. Christ says nothing. The Inquisitor explains that Christ made a fatal mistake in giving human beings freedom. In the wilderness temptations (bread, miracle, authority), Christ refused to turn stones into bread, refused to perform spectacular signs on demand, and refused worldly power. In doing so, he affirmed human freedom—freedom to love, freedom to choose, freedom to believe without coercion.

The Inquisitor argues that this was too much for humanity. People, he says, do not want freedom. They want bread, security, and someone to tell them what to believe. They want miracle, mystery, and authority. So the Church, in his telling, has corrected Christ’s work. It has taken up the sword of Caesar. It gives the people certainty and structure. It relieves them of the burden of freedom. And in doing so, it makes them happy.

The Inquisitor is not simply a villain. He is an argument. He represents the claim that human beings crave security more than truth, order more than freedom. Authority, in this view, is justified because it stabilizes fragile humanity.

We often celebrate modern science as liberating: freeing us from superstition, from arbitrary rule, from ignorance. And that is true in many respects. But large-scale scientific and technological systems also generate new forms of authority—expert systems, bureaucracies, metrics, algorithms. They promise bread (material progress), miracle (technological wonder), and authority (data-driven certainty).

The Inquisitor believes he loves humanity. He thinks he is merciful. He believes people are too weak to bear the weight of freedom. That is part of what makes the story unsettling. Authority can arise not only from domination, but from paternalism—from the conviction that others cannot handle the truth.

You are training to enter professions that wield real authority—medicine, engineering, data science, research. You will have expertise others do not. Will the authority that you wield be secure enough to respect the freedom of others? When confronted with dehumanizing technologies and philosophies, will you "kiss the Inquisitor"? Will you have the strength to disengage from destructive argumentation, affirm your own freedom, and walk out the open door to your vocational calling?

Enjoy the reading by Max Weber. See you Thursday.

The is/ought problem, Hume's Law, and pushback from Eric Fromm

Great conversation today. The idea that you cannot logically derive a moral conclusion (“ought”) purely from descriptive facts (“is”) without adding some normative premise comes most famously from David Hume, an 18th-century Scottish philosopher. This became known as the “is–ought problem” (or “Hume’s Law”).

I ended class with the idea that we consider pushing back on this conception, in spite of the fact that our course began with Max Weber emphasizing the "is-ought" distinction.

After some interacting with generative AI, it is apparenent that I am not alone. In fact, Eric Fromm (a psychoanalysist from the Frankfurt School), pushes back against a strict fact/value split in an interesting way.

Fromm accepts the logical point of Hume's Law, but then argues that human nature itself carries built-in normative implications—so the gap isn’t as wide as Hume thought.

Fromm thinks humans have a specific kind of nature—we’re not blank slates. We have inherent needs:

  • for relatedness (love, connection)
  • for rootedness and identity
  • for creativity and meaningful activity
  • for transcendence (going beyond mere survival)

These aren’t just preferences—they’re conditions for psychological health.

Eric Fromm argues something like:

  • If humans are this kind of being (an “is” about human nature)
  • Then certain ways of living help us flourish, and others distort us
  • Therefore, we can make grounded claims about how we ought to live

In other words, he tries to build an ethics of human flourishing rooted in what we are. Eric Fromm writes about this in Man for Himself, where he calls his view “humanistic ethics.”

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Great conversation today.   The idea that you cannot logically derive a moral conclusion (“ought”) purely from descriptive facts (“is”) without adding some normative premise comes most famously from <strong>David Hume</strong>, an 18th-century Scottish philosopher.  This became known as the “<strong>is–ought problem</strong>” (or “<strong>Hume’s Law</strong>”). </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>I ended class with the idea that we consider pushing back on this conception, in spite of the fact that our course began with Max Weber emphasizing the "is-ought" distinction. </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>After some interacting with generative AI, it is apparenent that I am not alone.  In fact,<strong> Eric Fromm </strong>(a psychoanalysist from the Frankfurt School), pushes back against a strict fact/value split in an interesting way.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Fromm <em>accepts the logical point</em> of Hume's Law, but then argues that <strong>human nature itself carries built-in normative implications</strong>—so the gap isn’t as wide as Hume thought.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Fromm thinks humans have a specific kind of nature—we’re not blank slates. We have inherent needs:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>for relatedness (love, connection)</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>for rootedness and identity</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>for creativity and meaningful activity</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>for transcendence (going beyond mere survival)</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>These aren’t just preferences—they’re <em>conditions for psychological health</em>.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Eric Fromm argues something like:</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:list -->
<ul class="wp-block-list"><!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>If humans are this kind of being (<em>an “is” about human nature</em>)</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>Then certain ways of living help us flourish, and others distort us</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item -->

<!-- wp:list-item -->
<li>Therefore, we can make grounded claims about how we <em>ought</em> to live</li>
<!-- /wp:list-item --></ul>
<!-- /wp:list -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>In other words, he tries to build an ethics of human flourishing rooted in what we <em>are</em>. Eric Fromm writes about this in <em>Man for Himself</em>, where he calls his view “<strong>humanistic ethics</strong>.”</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
